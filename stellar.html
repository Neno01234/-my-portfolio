How Stars Die

A star spends most of its life in a delicate balance: the outward pressure from nuclear fusion in its core fights the inward pull of gravity. When the fuel runs out, gravity wins. What happens next depends almost entirely on the star’s initial mass.

Low-mass stars gently shed their outer layers and leave behind a dense white dwarf. More massive stars undergo catastrophic core collapse, triggering a supernova and leaving either a neutron star or, if the remnant is heavy enough, a black hole.

1. Fuel Exhaustion

Hydrogen fusion ceases in the core. The core contracts and heats up, igniting heavier elements in shells around it. The star expands into a red giant or supergiant.

2. Core Collapse

When iron is produced in the most massive stars, fusion no longer releases energy. Electron degeneracy or neutron degeneracy can temporarily halt the collapse — or fail completely.

3. Remnant Formation

The final object is determined by whether electron degeneracy pressure, neutron degeneracy pressure, or neither can support the remaining mass against gravity.

Key Physics

Iron core exceeds Chandrasekhar mass and collapses. Neutron degeneracy + nuclear forces halt the collapse at ~10–15 km radius. Shock wave drives a supernova.

The Three Possible Endpoints

⚪ White Dwarf

Progenitor ≲ 8 M☉

Electron degeneracy pressure supports the remnant. Typical mass ~0.6 M☉, size comparable to Earth. The outer layers are ejected as a beautiful planetary nebula. Our Sun will become a white dwarf in about 5 billion years.

Limit: Chandrasekhar mass ≈ 1.4 M☉

🟠 Neutron Star

Progenitor ~8–25 M☉

After a spectacular core-collapse supernova, the core is crushed to nuclear densities. Neutrons provide the degeneracy pressure. A teaspoon of neutron-star material would weigh billions of tons. Many become pulsars.

Limit: TOV mass ≈ 2.0–2.5 M☉ (non-rotating)

🔴 Black Hole

Progenitor ≳ 25 M☉

When even neutron degeneracy fails, the core collapses past the event horizon. Nothing, not even light, can escape. Stellar-mass black holes typically range from ~5 to several tens of solar masses, depending on mass loss and fallback.

Schwarzschild radius Rs = 2GM/c² ≈ 2.95 km × (M/M☉)

The Physics of the Final Collapse

Electron Degeneracy (White Dwarfs)

Pauli exclusion principle forces electrons into higher momentum states as density rises. This quantum pressure is independent of temperature. When the core mass exceeds ~1.4 M☉, electrons become ultra-relativistic and the pressure can no longer support the star → collapse continues.

Neutron Degeneracy (Neutron Stars)

At nuclear densities (~10¹⁴ g/cm³), protons and electrons combine into neutrons. Neutron degeneracy pressure (plus strong nuclear repulsion) can support objects up to the Tolman–Oppenheimer–Volkoff limit. Beyond that, collapse is inevitable.

Event Horizon & Singularity

Once the collapsing core’s radius falls inside its Schwarzschild radius, an event horizon forms. From the outside, time appears to freeze at the horizon. Inside, general relativity predicts a singularity — a region of infinite curvature where the known laws of physics break down.

Rs = 2GM / c² ≈ 2.95 (M / M☉) km

For a 10 M☉ black hole the event horizon is only ~30 km across — smaller than a large city, yet containing ten times the mass of the Sun.

Photon Sphere Dynamics

Just outside the event horizon lies the photon sphere at r = 1.5 Rs (for a non-spinning black hole). Here, gravity is strong enough that light can travel in unstable circular orbits.

rphoton = 3GM / c² = 1.5 Rs

These orbits are unstable: a tiny inward perturbation and the photon falls through the horizon; a tiny outward one and it escapes to infinity. Photons that skim this region can loop several times, creating the bright photon ring seen in Event Horizon Telescope images. In the simulator, colored light trails show this behavior — warm trails spiral in (capture), cool trails deflect away (scatter), and pale trails temporarily orbit.

Important Caveats

  • Mass thresholds are approximate and depend on metallicity, rotation, and binary interactions. Real stars lose significant mass through winds.
  • Very massive stars (≳100–150 M☉) can encounter pair-instability, which may completely disrupt the star or leave an intermediate-mass black hole.
  • The simulation uses simplified educational thresholds: <8 M☉ → white dwarf, 8–25 M☉ → neutron star, >25 M☉ → black hole.
  • Modern supernova simulations show a complex “landscape” of successful explosions versus failed supernovae even within the same mass range.
